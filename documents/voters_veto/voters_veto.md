# The Voters’ Veto: Local Racial Demographic Change and Exclusionary Behavior

### Michael Hankinson

### May 27, 2026

Abstract

As Western democracies diversify, racial threat risks activating exclusionary attitudes among members of the majority. While research has focused on support for immigration restrictions, local housing policy is the primary means of maintaining racial segregation in the US. Using referendum vote share from over 3,700 precincts in Los Angeles County, I show that an increase in the local non-white population is associated with greater behavioral support for direct democratic control over affordable housing. I argue that this “voters’ veto” is valued for excluding low-income, predominantly nonwhite residents from one’s community. This relationship exists across majority white, Black, and Latino precincts, demonstrating that exclusionary reactions are not limited to racial threat among the majority. Instead, a shrinking white population may signal shared concerns about neighborhood trajectory. Given local conditions can shape po-

litical beliefs, these findings provide a local mechanism for the shift of both white and

minority voters towards exclusionary political movements.

The United States and other Western democracies are rapidly racially diversifying, largely due to immigration (Frey 2018). According to theories of racial threat, a growing minority population may present an economic, cultural, and political threat to the majority group (Blalock 1967; Blumer 1958), eliciting exclusionary attitudes and behaviors among its members (Craig, Rucker and Richeson 2018; Kaufmann and Goodwin 2018). However, three overlapping limitations have hampered theory in this space.

First, researchers have focused on the exclusionary responses of non-Hispanic whites (e.g., Abrajano and Hajnal 2015; Fouka and Tabellini 2022; Outten et al. 2012; Reny and Newman 2018; Sahn 2025), risking a majority-minority dichotomy that overlooks the motivations of Black, Latino, and Asian voters. Where minority attitudes have been examined, research has largely relied on static distributions of group size rather than the dynamic process of demographic change (e.g., Bobo and Hutchings 1996; Gay 2006; Oliver and Wong 2003). Second, work on demographic change has measured either attitudes or behaviors that only indirectly shape policy—turnout and candidate choice (e.g., Enos 2016; Newman, Shah and Collingwood 2018; Hill, Hopkins and Huber 2019)—rather than direct behavioral support for exclusionary policy itself. Third, studies that do measure policy preferences emphasize national outcomes like immigration (e.g., Enos 2014; Hopkins 2010), even though a voters’ local exposure to demographic change in the U.S. is heavily shaped by municipal zoning. “Exclusionary zoning” establishes a local price floor that blocks lower-income, disproportionately non-white residents (Einstein, Glick and Palmer 2020; Rothwell and Massey 2009; Trounstine 2020). The few studies linking demographic change to local exclusionary policy focus on citywide institutional outcomes attributable to white homeowners (e.g., Newman 2013; Sahn 2025), leaving the more recently diversifying communities under-examined.

I address these limitations using a California law known as “Article 34” which requires any new “low-rent” housing project to win majority approval at municipal ballot.1 Article 34 transferred decision-making over affordable housing from city councils to local electorates, 1Though initially targeted towards public housing, “low-rent housing” has been interpreted to include any housing in which 50% or more of the units are government-subsidized.

and the California legislature has tried three times to repeal it via statewide vote (1974, 1980, 1993). The 1993 effort offers a rare opportunity to observe voters’ behavioral support for an exclusionary policy tool: direct veto power over new affordable housing.

Using returns from 3,718 precincts across Los Angeles County, I find that a standard deviation increase in a precinct’s non-white population from 1980 to 1993 is associated with a 4.5 percentage point (d = .32) increase in support for this “voters’ veto.” Results are similar when change is operationalized as a proportion, and are not explained by changes in density, residential churn, median income, or percent foreign born. This exclusionary response emerges in majority white, majority Black, and majority Latino precincts alike. If anything, the response to a declining local white population is larger in non-white precincts, and is not driven by a decline in any minority group’s own size. To address the possibility that new voters rather than incumbent residents drive the result, I show that among precincts with minimal residential churn and minimal demographic change of their own, spatial proximity to a rapidly diversifying neighborhood predicts higher support for the voters’ veto (+3.5 points, d = .24).

Together, these findings suggest that exclusionary reactions to local demographic change are not limited to the traditionally understood majority group (i.e., whites in the U.S.), nor reducible to intergroup competition. Instead, a shrinking white population may signal shared concerns about a neighborhood’s political, economic, and social trajectory, what Pattillo- McCoy (1999, p. 6) describes as middle-class Black neighborhoods working to “fortify” themselves against encroachment from poorer communities. The implications are twofold. As minorities increasingly move to suburbs (Badger, Bui and Gebeloff 2019), voters across racial backgrounds may protect existing exclusionary zoning, deepening the affordability crisis. And given that local conditions shape social identities and partisanship (e.g., Cramer 2016; Larsen et al. 2019; Ternullo 2024), the local mechanism documented here may help explain the broader movement of Latino, Asian, and Black voters in high-immigration areas toward more exclusionary political coalitions (Cai and Fessenden 2020; Fraga, Velez and

West Forthcoming).

## Direct Democracy as an Exclusionary Institution

Direct democracy in the United States has long enabled voters to bypass legislative processes via ballot measure (Gerber 2011), and at the state and local level it has frequently been used to perpetuate housing segregation. In a 1964 California statewide election, voters in predominantly white cities near diversifying areas were more likely to support a measure permitting racial discrimination by sellers, landlords, and real estate agents (Reny and Newman 2018). Once de jure segregation was prohibited, voters constructed economic barriers instead: single-family zoning sets a minimum price of residency, and municipalities near center cities that received greater mid-century Black migration were more likely to adopt it (Sahn 2025; Trounstine 2018). This use of policy to economically “defend” one’s community is known as “exclusionary zoning” (Einstein, Glick and Palmer 2020).

I focus on one such tool: direct democratic control over affordable housing. While the institution appears race-neutral, its origins underscore the tension between racial demographic change and the voters’ veto. Following the Housing Act of 1949—which promised 810,000 new units alongside the Second Great Migration—residents in the Northern California city of Eureka spearheaded a constitutional amendment letting voters stop their local housing authority from building low-income housing (Cavin 2019; Varian 2022). Partnering with the California Real Estate Association, the campaign pitched the amendment as essential to countering “minority pressure groups” and preserving white neighborhoods (Staff 2019), while the official voter guide framed it as a vote “neither for nor against public housing” but for “the democratic process of government” (Voter Information Guide for 1950, General Election 1950). The amendment passed narrowly, and by 1968 voters had rejected nearly half of all proposed public housing statewide—roughly 15,000 units—with many agencies shelving projects rather than risk a vote (Cavin 2019). The U.S. Supreme Court upheld

Article 34, finding: “This procedure ensures that all the people of a community will have a voice in a decision which may lead to large expenditures of local revenues. It gives them a voice in decisions that will affect the future development of their own community” (James v. Valtierra, 402 U.S. 137 (1971)). At the justices’ private conference, Chief Justice Warren Burger allegedly scoffed at the plaintiff’s claims, framing their argument as a suggestion that “too much democracy violates the Equal Protection Clause” (Cavin 2019).

Subsequent repeal attempts at the ballot box failed in 1974 and 1980, with pro-Article 34 campaigns emphasizing taxes and local democracy rather than race. By the third repeal effort in 1993, public commentary connected Article 34 to community character and quality of life. State Assembly Member Gebe Ferguson, for example, framed support for the voters’ veto as unrelated to race and shared across racial lines, claiming that “low-income black communities don’t want low-income housing built in their communities either” (Martinez 1993). While Ferguson likely extrapolated about Black preferences, affordable housing has been historically stigmatized through stereotypes about its occupants (Tighe 2010). If voters view direct democracy over housing as a means to exclude poor minority residents, then the veto power granted by Article 34 would be a valuable backstop against not just affordable housing, but local racial demographic change.

What shapes voter support for direct democracy? Voters are often only “weakly principled,” caring more about policy outcomes than process (Prothro and Grigg 1960), and so may protect direct democracy when they believe it will produce their preferred outcomes. A primary threat to a majority group’s preferred outcomes is demographic change, which can threaten the status quo power structure (Blalock 1967; Blumer 1958) and generate a broad anxiety over the loss of economic, cultural, and political power (e.g., Abrajano and Hajnal 2015; Thompson 2023). Absent the conditions for positive intergroup contact (Allport 1954),

a sudden increase in the local non-white population is therefore expected to elicit a desire among white voters to retain direct democracy as a means of ensuring exclusionary policy. Demographic change can occur at national, regional, or local scales, with each plausibly evoking different mechanisms (e.g., identity and federal politics; media coverage and crime; home values and neighborhood trajectory). Local change should most closely drive exclusionary behavior in the case of Article 34, where the policy lever itself is local.

### Demographic Change in Los Angeles County, 1993

From 1980 to 1993, Los Angeles County saw little growth in its Black population but large increases in Latino migration and a proportionally large increase in Asian migration. Simultaneously, the non-Hispanic white share fell from 53 to 38 percent, and the median Los Angeles County voter experienced a 14 percentage point (54 percent) increase in their precinct’s non-white population. For racial threat to operate, voters must perceive these changes (Hopkins 2009; Wong 2007). A 1992 survey found that 56 percent of Los Angeles County residents felt their neighborhood was changing ethnically (Bobo and Zubrinsky 1996). Because Latino and Asian arrivals concentrated in historically Black areas (Bergesen and Herman 1998), Black residents were the most likely to perceive change (78 percent).

Local demographic change may drive exclusionary behavior through two pathways. The first is concern about what a shrinking white share signals for one’s neighborhood. Members of many racial groups show some ingroup preference (Charles 2006; Farley et al. 1994; Krysan et al. 2009), but there may also be a shared preference for white neighbors rooted in concerns over political and socioeconomic trajectory. For example, white constituents tend to receive better representation, responsiveness, and public goods from local government (e.g., Hankinson and Magazinnik 2023; Schaffner, Rhodes and La Raja 2020). Contemporaneous evidence is supportive. Charles (2006)’s Los Angeles Study of Urban Inequality, fielded in 1992–1993, found a consistent racial rank-ordering of preferred out-group neighbors across racial groups, with whites always most preferred and Blacks least preferred—even among

non-white respondents. Pattillo-McCoy (1999, p. 6) similarly describes middle-class Black families working to “fortify their neighborhoods against this encroachment” from poorer, predominantly minority areas.

The second pathway is perceived competition among racial groups for space and resources (Bobo and Hutchings 1996; Gay 2006; McClain and Karnig 1990). Charles (2006, p. 164) finds that Black Angelenos’ preferences for Asian neighbors are negatively shaped by the belief that Asians pose a competitive economic and political threat. This tension was acute in early 1990s Los Angeles. Eighteen months before the 1993 Article 34 vote, the acquittal of four officers in the Rodney King beating triggered rioting concentrated in South Central and Koreatown, in which Black and Latino rioters targeted the Korean community amid resentment over labor-market competition and political underrepresentation (Johnson Jr and Farrell Jr 1992). Bergesen and Herman (1998) attribute the intensity of the riots specifically to neighborhood racial succession, with areas of greatest demographic change exhibiting the highest violence.

In short, while existing literature largely associates racial demographic change with racial threat among white voters, local change may also provoke exclusionary behavior among non-white voters—either through shared concerns about what a shrinking white population implies for a community’s trajectory, or through perceived competition with arriving minority groups. Through both pathways, increases in the local non-white population may drive support for the voters’ veto across racial backgrounds.

## Internal Demographic Change

I use two analytical strategies. First, I assess the link between internal precinct demographic change from 1980 to 1993 and support for repealing Article 34 in November 1993. Second, I subset to precincts that experienced minimal residential churn and were racially stable over the period, and measure the relationship between spatial proximity to a rapidly diversifying

Census tract and support for repealing the voters’ veto. Results are substantively similar across both strategies, and exclusionary responses appear in majority Black and majority Latino precincts as well as majority white ones.

### Data

I combine precinct-level returns from the 1993 election with tract-level Census data to generate 3,718 precinct observations in Los Angeles County. Los Angeles is the only county for which I located a contemporaneous voter file—common resources lack precinct returns and digitized boundaries from this period—but it provides a uniquely dense multiracial context and, with 9 million residents in 1993, a substantively meaningful share of California’s electorate. Because no shapefile of 1993 precinct boundaries exists, I use Enos, Kaufman and Sands (2019) geocoded 1992 voter file to allocate tract-level Census quantities (Manson et al. 2022) to precincts in proportion to each precinct’s share of tract voters.

Dependent Variable The dependent variable is the precinct-level vote share supporting the voters’ veto—that is, the complement of support for repealing Article 34. The weighted mean was 55.4 percent in favor of the veto power. Figure B-1 shows that support was concentrated outside the more urbanized parts of the county, and tracks party registration (Figure B-2).

Independent Variables The treatment is local racial demographic change in the leadup to the 1993 election. Because the election predates the American Community Survey, I linearly interpolate between the 1990 and 2000 Censuses to estimate 1993 values. I operationalize change in two ways—percentage point change and proportion change—because theory does not privilege one; the two are correlated at 0.65 and capture related but distinct aspects of demographic turnover (Section C). The population-weighted median precinct saw a 14 percentage point (54 percent) increase in non-white population over the period. Because aggregating tracts into precincts introduces measurement error that compounds across the

two time points, I winsorize the treatment at the 5th and 95th percentiles; non-winsorized results are substantively identical (Table H-9).

### Empirical Strategy

I regress precinct-level vote share supporting the voters’ veto on the 1980–1993 change in non-white population, with both treatments standardized for comparability. The identifying assumption is selection on observables. The granularity of within-county variation rules out shift-share approaches, which require county-level migration data (Borusyak, Hull and Jaravel 2025).

All models include 1980 pretreatment controls for percent non-Hispanic white, homeownership rate, vacancy rate, log median household income, population density, and unemployment rate—observable conditions that may be associated with both subsequent racial change and contemporaneous economic anxiety. Results are substantively identical when I additionally include the 1980–1993 change in each control (Table D-1), though such timevarying controls risk post-treatment bias if they are themselves affected by the demographic change (Sen and Wasow 2016). I include Census place fixed effects to absorb time-invariant place attributes, weight by precinct population, and cluster Huber-White standard errors at the place level to account for correlated treatment within places.

### Mechanisms by Racial Groups

If the relationship reflects racial threat as conventionally theorized, it should be concentrated in majority white precincts. To test this, I interact the treatment with indicators for whether a precinct was majority white, majority Black, or majority Latino in 1980 (372 majority Black and 401 majority Latino precincts; majority Asian precincts, n = 8, lack statistical power for separate estimation).

Figure 1 shows the opposite of the standard racial-threat prediction. Majority Black and Latino precincts exhibit increases in support for the voters’ veto that are at least as large aa those in majority white precincts. The exclusionary response to a declining local white population is not confined to majority white precincts.

Two further tests sharpen the mechanism. First, I estimate the association of an increase in each minority group separately, including all groups simultaneously with lagged 1980 levels (Figure 2). ## Proximity to Demographic Change

One threat to the within-precinct results is compositional. Support for the voters’ veto may be rising in diversifying precincts not because incumbent voters are responding, but because new voters are arriving. Two pieces of context make this unlikely. First, precincts with more non-white voters on average show lower support for the voters’ veto (Figure B-3), so new non-white arrivals would have to be voting against type to drive the result. Second, exit in response to local demographic change is more likely among the least racially tolerant—the voters most supportive of the veto. If anything, compositional change should attenuate the observed relationship.

To address this concern directly, I use a second identification strategy that does not rely on within-precinct racial change as the treatment. Following Reny and Newman (2018), I measure each precinct’s proximity to a rapidly diversifying Census tract. Among racially stable, low-churn precincts, those closer to diversifying neighborhoods were more supportive of the voters’ veto than those farther away—evidence that residential turnover is unlikely to explain the results in the previous section.

### Data and Empirical Strategy

Using the same precinct data, I calculate each precinct’s proximity to a “diversifying tract“: a Census tract whose non-white population share increased substantially from 1980 to 1993. Defining “substantial” involves a trade-off, since too low a threshold may capture imperceptible change and too high a threshold sacrifices statistical power. My main specification defines diversifying tracts as those above the 90th percentile of non-white population increase — a >27 percentage point increase, or a >127 percent proportional increase. Results are substantively similar at the 85th and 95th percentiles (Tables I-10 to I-13). To ensure changes are likely to be perceptible, I subset to tracts above the bottom tercile of popula-

tion (>3,136 residents); Figure C-7 maps the resulting set under both definitions. Census tract boundaries do not perfectly match perceived neighborhoods (Wong 2007), but they are stable over time and the standard unit in studies of neighborhood racial change (Lee et al. 2008).

Proximity is the negative distance between each precinct’s centroid and its nearest diversifying tract, standardized so the treatment is a standard deviation increase in proximity (approximately 3.5 kilometers). I regress precinct vote share supporting the voters’ veto on proximity, with the same 1980 pretreatment covariates as the previous design (percent non-Hispanic white, homeownership rate, log median household income, vacancy rate, unemployment rate, and population density) Census place fixed effects, population weights, and Huber-White standard errors clustered at the level of the nearest diversifying tract.

### Results

To isolate exposure to nearby change from within-precinct change, I subset to precincts in the bottom tercile of both residential churn and own racial change from 1980 to 1993. In these precincts, the non-white population rose by less than 6.5 percentage points (median +3 points) under the percentage-point definition, or less than 27 percent (median +4 percent) under the proportional definition.

## Discussion

Research on neighborhood racial change typically frames intergroup encounters as either contact or conflict. Oliver and Wong (2003) find that minorities living near one another tend to hold lower outgroup prejudice, while Enos (2017) shows that the salience of group identity rises with relative group size, spatial proximity, and segregation, raising the prospect of conflict absent the conditions for positive contact outline in Allport (1954).

My findings neither emphasize contact nor conflict. Rather, they suggest that local racial demographic change can activate broadly shared concerns about a neighborhood’s political and socioeconomic trajectory that are attributed to racial turnover in general. A desire to protect the voters’ veto is therefore not equivalent to heightened racial prejudice. For instance, I would not expect to find a parallel relationship between local demographic change and support for racially targeted statewide ballot measures such as restrictions on public services for undocumented immigrants (e.g., Campbell, Wong and Citrin 2006). But even when not driven by overt racial threat, behavioral support for local exclusionary policy can produce the same outcomes as if it were, entrenching existing patterns of segregation. Two scope conditions are worth emphasizing. First, the result that majority Black and Latino precincts respond to a declining local white population—and not to declines in their own group’s share—pushes against a simple group-threat extension to minority contexts. Whatever drives the response, it is not symmetric ingroup defense. Second, although the within-precinct and proximity-based designs converge on the same substantive finding, neither rules out every alternative. Local crime, in particular, remains a plausible confounder that I cannot directly test given the absence of neighborhood-level crime data prior to 2000. Researchers with more recent outcomes should examine it directly.

Finally, the geographic setting matters. Los Angeles County in 1993 was uniquely dense, uniquely diverse, and uniquely shaped by the Second Great Migration and post-1965 immigration. Whether the same exclusionary response would emerge in less dense, more recently diversifying suburban or exurban contexts, where minority arrivals are a more novel phe-

nomenon and intergroup contact opportunities are more constrained, is an open question.

## Conclusion

Three limitations have shaped existing work on demographic change and exclusionary politics: a focus on white voters, a reliance on attitudes or indirect behaviors rather than direct policy support, and an emphasis on national rather than local outcomes. This study addresses each by examining a rare instance of direct behavioral support for a local exclusionary tool—the voters’ veto over affordable housing under California’s Article 34. Across 3,718 Los Angeles County precincts and two analytical strategies, local exposure to an increasing non-white population from 1980 to 1993 is associated with greater support for that veto, and this exclusionary response appears in majority white, Black, and Latino precincts alike. The implications are timely. Los Angeles County in 1993 closely resembles California as a whole in 2020 demographically, and the state continues to diversify: California’s white population fell 24 percent from 2010 to 2020, the largest statewide decline. In November 2024, Democratic leaders pulled a fourth attempt to repeal Article 34 from the ballot after polling showed it underwater (Board 2024). Given the similarities in demographic trends leading up to 1993 and 2024, advocates may have been wise to pull the measure and avoid a rebuke of the state’s efforts to limit local control over affordable housing. Meanwhile, a countermovement, “Our Neighborhood Voices,” is organizing a competing initiative to end state preemption of local land use policy (Brasuell 2022). While hopes to loosen local control as a response to both segregation and the housing affordability crisis hang on scaling decision-making up to the state level, the findings here suggest local demographic change pulls voters in the opposite direction.

Currently, Americans harboring exclusionary attitudes—either on national immigration policy or local zoning practices—are more likely to find their policy preferences embraced by the Republican Party. As the United States diversifies, there is growing evidence that

many Latino, Asian, and to a lesser extent Black voters may be to moving away from the Democratic Party, especially in areas with high levels of immigration. The exclusionary behaviors found in this study align with and may provide a local foundation for this national shift of non-white voters towards the Republican Party. More broadly, in the context of the nation’s housing affordability crisis, these findings underscore the tension between integration and local democratic control over housing in a diversifying society.

## A Article 34 Background

Article 34 of the California Constitution:

“No low rent housing project shall hereafter be developed, constructed, or acquired in any manner by any state public body until, a majority of the qualified electors of the city, town or county, as the case may be, in which it is proposed to develop, construct, or acquire the same, voting upon such issue, approve such project by voting in favor thereof at an election to be held for that purpose, or at any general or special election.”

1993 ballot measure to repeal Article 34 (Proposition 168):

“LOW RENT HOUSING PROJECTS. LEGISLATIVE CONSTITUTIONAL AMENDMENT. Amends state constitutional definition of low rent housing projects to include only projects owned by a governmental entity as defined. Excludes projects found to have no significant negative impact on the revenues of the affected governmental entity, and whose physical appearance is found to have no significant negative impact on the surrounding community. Requires approval by voters only upon qualification of ballot petition as specified. Exempts projects approved on or before November 3, 1992, or projects with existing contracts for federal financial assistance.”

In short, most of the variation in the percentage point change in population occurs in areas between 50 and 80 percent white, whereas most of the variation in proportion change is in areas that are greater than 80 percent white circa 1980.

### D.1 Intergroup Competition

To test whether the main relationship is driven by either a concern over losing white residents or rather a decrease in one’s own racial group which may stimulate group threat among minorities, I test whether majority Black and Latino precincts show a similar increase in support for the voters’ veto when their own racial group decreases in size. These coefficients are also not standardized. The percentage point change model is based on moving from 0 percent to 100 percent of that ethnic group, whereas the proportion model is defined as a 100 percent increase in group size.

Isolating these majority Black and majority Latino precincts, I find that a rise in the non-Black and non-Latino populations, respectively does not elicit an exclusionary reaction (Tables D-2 and D-3). Only a decrease in the local white population spurs support for local control over affordable housing. These subgroup analyses suggest that voters from an array of racial and ethnic backgrounds may feel concerns over the economic, social, and political trajectory of their neighborhoods as the local white population shrinks, rather than concerns over the size and influence of their own racial group.

## E Spatial Durbin Error Model

Spatial autocorrelation can be a problem when using geographic data due to the data’s violation of SUTVA. Neighboring observations can influence each other, violating SUTVA’s independence assumption. Likewise, omitted variables may be spatially correlated. Fortunately, these relationships can be modeled.

Using a spatial model requires two steps: the definition of spatial relationships and the choice of a model to account for spatial dependence. The most common way to model spatial relationships is by using queen contiguity weights. These weights account for spatial interactions between units sharing either a border or a vertex. Contiguity weights are especially helpful for the precinct data used in this analysis because the relationships are not sensitive to absolute distance. For example, some parts of Los Angeles County are very dense, where spillovers may only extend 500 meters. In contrast, interactions between precincts in rural Los Angeles County may occur at the level of several kilometers. By defining spatial interactions at the level of shared precinct borders (i.e., a queen continguity weights matrix), this design accounts for variation in the scale of human interaction across Los Angeles County. To select a model, I considered how spatial dependence may be affecting the analysis. First, the treatment of demographic change is likely to be affecting neighboring precincts. In fact, the second analysis of this study is based on exposure to nearby diversifying precincts. Thus, the model would need to account for this spillover effect. Second, there are likely omitted variables which may be spatially correlated. A spatial Durbin error model (SDEM) accounts for the spatial spillover effects of covariates as well as autocorrelation in the error term (LeSage 2014).

I reproduce the results from the Table 1 using the SDEM. Due to the computational intensity of incorporating the spatial autocorrelation, adjustments were made to the model specifications. Models 1 and 3 include Census place-level fixed effects, but no covariates. Models 2 and 4 include covariates, but no fixed effects. The covariates in Models 2 and 4 are the same as those in the non-spatial results: the 1980 precinct-level percent nonwhite residents, homeownership rate, vacancy rate, population density, log median household income, and unemployment rate. The results are substantively similar across all four models. All models use the queen continuity weights matrix and are weighted by precinct population. Having accounted for spatial spillovers and spatially correlated error terms, a standard deviation increase in the local non-white population increases support for direct democracy over housing by 2.0 to 2.2 percentage points. When defined as a proportion increase, the effect is a 0.8 to 2.8 point increase in support for the voters’ veto over affordable housing. These effects substantively match the results from the non-spatial OLS model in Table 1.
