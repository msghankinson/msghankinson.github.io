# The Resilient, but Limited Effect of Process on Fairness and Legitimacy: Evidence from Wind Energy

### Michael Hankinson

### April 8, 2026

Abstract

Fair processes increase outcome acceptance. But if legitimacy is mainly driven by whether one receives their preferred outcome, then how do voters evaluate procedural fairness prior to knowing the outcome? Do voters rely on sincere procedural values or strategic considerations regarding which process will lead to their preferred outcome? Using preregistered survey experiments in the context of wind energy siting, I vary the decision-making authority, level of public input, and whether respondents learn the outcome before evaluating the process. Greater public input and local control increase perceived fairness and legitimacy. Importantly, these effects are resilient to learning the policy outcome. Process evaluations are not simply ex ante strategic forecasts of outcomes—they are stable preferences. Still, receiving one’s preferred outcome has a larger effect on ex post evaluations compared to process alone. Thus, reforms which centralize energy siting are unlikely to lose many would-be supporters on the basis of

procedural fairness.

Voters regularly experience political outcomes with which they disagree. But those who believe that the decision-making process was fair are more willing to weather political losses and maintain democratic norms compared to those who feel cheated (Lind and Tyler 1988; Thibaut and Walker 1975; Tyler 1994). At the same, research finds that attitudes towards process alone are weak and voters are quick abandon procedural values in favor of preferred outcomes (Prothro and Grigg 1960). In fact, when evaluating process retrospectively, voters mainly base their evaluations of procedural fairness and legitimacy on whether they received their preferred outcome (Esaiasson et al. 2016).

But if ex post evaluations are driven by outcomes, then how do voters evaluate the process prior to learning the outcome? On one hand, “sincere procedural values” are those resilient to knowledge of the outcome. A citizen who genuinely values a process should maintain their evaluation regardless of the outcome. These sincere procedural values are the foundation for legitimizing unpopular outcomes, from judicial decisions (Bartels and Johnston 2020; Gibson, Caldeira and Baird 1998), to election outcomes (Anderson 2005), and to public policies (Grimes 2006; Tyler, Casper and Fisher 1989). On the other hand, “strategic considerations” are built around the likelihood of securing one’s preferred outcome. If based on strategic considerations, the effect of process should attenuate once the outcome is revealed.

Capturing sincere procedural values requires isolating the effect of process independent of the outcome. This is challenging. Most observational evidence of procedural fairness is retrospective, measuring perceptions of fairness after a decision is known (e.g., Firestone et al. 2018, 2020; Gross 2007; Hoen et al. 2019; Hoesch et al. 2025). Consequently, outcome favorability is likely to bias evaluations of procedural fairness. Experimental designs also face limitations. Varying the process can affect respondents’ perceptions of the most likely outcome. Thus, experiments that randomize the process but do not specify the outcome cannot isolate the effect of process (e.g., Beyers and Arras 2021; Jacobs and Kaufmann 2021; Liu et al. 2020; Persson, Esaiasson and Gilljam 2013; Wicki, Hofer and Kaufmann

2022).1

At stake is our interpretation of evidence on procedural fairness. If pre-outcome procedural evaluations are strategic, then much of this evidence is biased—capturing expectations of outcomes as well as any legitimizing effect of the process. Unpacking this bias is also crucial for institutional design. In line with procedural fairness theory, 20th century reforms in North America and Europe granted more voice and authority over the development of critical infrastructure to the local level (Altshuler and Luberoff 2004). Because such infrastructure has spatially concentrated costs, local control empowers opponents to block development even if there are more net supporters (Einstein, Glick and Palmer 2019; Sahn 2025). If pre-outcome testimony on procedural fairness is driven by strategic implications, then these reforms not only constrain the supply of collective goods—from housing (Hankinson and Magazinnik 2023; Mast 2024), to energy (Carley et al. 2020), to transportation infrastructure (Brooks and Liscow 2023)—but do little to increase the acceptance of unpopular outcomes.

Wind energy siting is an ideal context for evaluating the effect of sincere procedural values on fairness (i.e., the evaluation of the process itself) and legitimacy (i.e., one’s willingness to accept the outcome). Wind energy infrastructure has diffuse benefits and spatially concentrated costs, ranging from concerns over aesthetic impacts to the preservation of local identity (Bergquist et al. 2020; Devine-Wright and Howes 2010; Susskind et al. 2022; Wolsink 2007). These costs drive powerful local opposition. Survey evidence finds that a third of recent wind energy projects have been canceled due to community protest (Nilson et al. 2024) and the rate of local protest is growing (Stokes et al. 2023). Even in Denmark, a country with majority public support for expansion of wind energy, opposition pushes local officials to site turbines in the least politically damaging areas (Andersen et al. 2025).

Across both experiments, three findings emerge. First, centralized decision making and limited public input reduce procedural fairness and the legitimacy of siting decisions when outcomes are unknown, with public input having the larger effect. Second, these effects are resilient to outcome information. Learning that a proposal is approved or denied does not diminish the independent effect of process on fairness and legitimacy. Third, although sincere procedural values matter, the effect of process alone is substantially smaller than the effect of receiving one’s preferred outcome.

Such reforms are unlikely to lose many would-be supporters on the basis of procedural fairness. Still, even modest average effects of process on legitimacy may be politically consequential if they are concentrated among a highly engaged, well-resourced faction willing to mobilize against the infrastructure. By isolating the effect of process from outcome, this study provides a framework for identifying such behavioral consequences in future work.

## Procedural Fairness, Legitimacy, and the Sincere-Strategic

## Distinction

The effect of process independent of outcome can be viewed through procedural fairness theory. The three-step theory begins with a decision being made using a procedure, such as the decision to build a piece of energy infrastructure. Next, an observer evaluates the fairness of the procedure. Finally, the observer’s assessment of fairness combined with their predisposition towards the infrastructure leads to either the acceptance or rejection of the decision—otherwise known as legitimacy (Tyler 1994).

For observational research, separating the effect of process from the effect of the outcome is challenging. Case studies and surveys build on retrospective correlations between the community engagement process and a sense of procedural fairness (Firestone et al. 2018, 2020; Gross 2007; Hoen et al. 2019; Hoesch et al. 2025) as well as support for the project overall (Nilson and Stedman 2023). For studies which only sample residents living near approved projects, researchers cannot observe projects which had processes deemed unfair enough that opponents derailed approval. Furthermore, retrospective reflections struggle to account for endogeneity. Recalled preferences are not only subject to measurement error, but also Tiebout-sorting (Tiebout 1956). A wind farm’s greatest opponents may be more likely to leave the area once siting is approved, whereas some supporters now living in the area were not present for the siting conflict.

Experimental research on the causal effect of procedural fairness has had inconsistent findings. On one hand, Martin, Miko(cid:32)lajczak and Orr (2022) randomly vary procedural fairness and find that a fair process increases legitimacy independent of project outcome. However, the study’s randomized violations of fairness include clear normative concerns, such as allegations of corruption in the process. It is unsurprising that a process labeled corrupt and “controversial” is seen as unfair. In contrast, Esaiasson et al. (2016) vary processes in policy areas ranging from regulation—a ban of religious symbols in schools—to

redistribution—the use of money for either charity or celebrations. Across 27 experiments, they find that outcome favorability dominates the effect of process when it comes to perceived fairness (Esaiasson et al. 2016).

Still, this “horse racing” of process versus outcome does not help us assess how voters evaluate procedural fairness prior to the outcome being revealed and whether those evaluations are sincere or strategic. Unpacking these mechanisms is particularly important given the evolving context of renewable energy siting. While most projects address regional needs, new infrastructure often has spatially concentrated costs, mobilizing local opposition. In response, research-driven recommendations for building local support have been greater local control and greater public input (e.g., Elmallah and Rand 2022; Firestone et al. 2018, 2020, 2012; Gadzanku et al. 2025; Gross 2007; Hoen et al. 2019; Hoesch et al. 2025). These reforms believed to not only produce projects better tailored to local conditions, but also increase the legitimacy of even unpopular projects. But if evaluations of procedural fairness are just strategic considerations to secure one’s preferred policy, then reforms which create more local veto points will not only constrain the supply of renewable energy, but do nothing to increase voters’ acceptance of unpopular siting decisions.

### Wind Turbine Siting Institutions

Isolating the relationship between institutions and procedural fairness is highly relevant for energy siting policy globally. Local governments typically control decision-making over new wind facilities, presenting obstacles to development even in countries where wind energy investment is popular, such as Denmark and Sweden (Andersen et al. 2025; Lindvall 2023; Vasstrøm et al. 2024). Compared to centralized authorities, local legislators are elected and thus incentivized to keep their votes more closely aligned with nearby residents’ attitudes. Additionally, local legislators will typically hold more in-person public hearings on wind proposals, facilitating a higher level of face-to-face public input.

In contrast to this local control with high public input, governments across North America

and Europe have sought to centralize decision-making authority. Recently, Germany curtailed local control by setting binding top-down wind energy targets and allowing the state and municipal authorities to manage planning and implementation. Additional reforms have removed public hearings for wind proposals unless requested by the developer or local authorities (Uibeleisen and Go¨tz 2024). The United Kingdom has similarly exempted large scale wind power installations from local planning approval. In contrast, Norway replaced local control over wind energy development with a national framework in 2009, sparking widespread protest. By 2019, Norway began a series of reforms to strengthen local control over wind siting, illustrating how a loss of legitimacy may threaten long-term policy success (Vasstrøm et al. 2024).

Rather than centralizing authority nationally, regional governments can also preempt local control. Canadian provinces have removed local veto authority, leading to backlash and the punishment of incumbent officials (Stokes 2016). In the U.S., states have also shifted decision-making over utility-scale wind and solar facilities from local governments to state-appointed boards of experts, including Illinois, California, New York, Wisconsin, and Michigan. Other states have had strong state control for much longer, including Connecticut, Maryland, West Virginia, and North Carolina. Another 20 states maintain a hybrid approach, where large-scale energy facilities and transmission lines are approved at the state level (Chaudhuri and Susskind 2024). While this preemption often requires at least one public hearing, the level of input typically falls short of that gathered through locally elected decision-making bodies.

## Analytical Approach

Prior to data collection, I preregistered two experimental designs. The surveys were fielded on samples of suburban and rural respondents in the United States. For clarity, I describe the common points of inquiry below. Complete preregistrations and all preregistered tests

are included in the Supplementary Information.

The experiments proposed a hypothetical wind farm within eyesight of a respondent’s neighborhood, highlighting the scale of the proposal as well as expected local benefits and costs commonly discussed in siting campaigns. The proposal randomly varied the level of government making the decision, the level of public input, the outcome, and whether respondents saw this outcome prior to evaluating the process. Dependent variables included the fairness of the decision-making process measured using a 7-item Likert scale and the legitimacy of the outcome. Legitimacy was measured using a novel index of four items designed to measure respondents’ acceptance of the decision.

Figure 1 displays a stylized survey flow common across both experiments. To test for sincere procedural values, the experiment uses two branches instead of one branch with preand post-outcome measures of fairness in order to avoid consistency bias. If respondents are less likely to change their answers to the same fairness asked before and after the outcome is revealed, then my results would be biased towards finding resilient effects of process. In contrast, randomizing respondents across two separate survey arms recovers the same conceptual estimates, but without any risk of consistency bias.

In this model, the treatment effect of process may operate through both sincere procedural values and strategic considerations.

## Methods

Experiment 1 was fielded in December 2024. Respondents were recruited via Cloud Research Connect, an opt-in survey platform found to have among the highest response quality compared to similar popular platforms (Stagnaro et al. 2024). After dropping respondents for failing pretreatment attention checks, I surveyed 1,995 respondents living in the United States, age 18 years or older. To maximize external validity, I excluded respondents living in urban areas, as a wind farm of 340 foot tall turbines is an unrealistic land use in that context. As a result, the sample has a larger share of homeowners and non-Hispanic white respondents compared to the United States as a whole. See SI Section 3 for full descriptive

statistics.

Experiment 2 was fielded in November 2025. I explicitly sampled respondents from rural areas in order to capture those most likely to encounter large-scale wind energy projects. Cloud Research Connect does not have a deep pool of rural respondents, so I fielded the survey via PureSpectrum, a platform which combines geographic micro-targeting with demographic quotas to recruit respondents from other opt-in survey providers. I used the University of Washington RUCA-to-ZCTA crosswalk, defining rural ZIP codes as those categorized with a RUCA code of 7 or higher (e.g., Nemerever and Rogers 2021). Demographic target quotas for income, race, homeownership, age, gender, and education are based on 2021-2025 5-year ACS estimates for these rural ZCTAs. I ultimately surveyed 3,076 respondents age 18 years or older, a sample size estimated to have 80% power to detect interactions as small as 5 percentage points—roughly half the size of the main effect in Experiment 1.

### Design

In both survey experiments, respondents were asked to evaluate a hypothetical wind farm of sixty 340 foot tall turbines proposed for their community. There were explicitly told that this was not a real proposal, but to thoughtfully consider it as if it were real. The prompt includes both arguments for and against the wind farm, as well as a visualization of the scale of the turbines. Due to the diverse rural geographies in the United States, this study was unable to incorporate photomontages that would represent each respondents’ surrounding context (e.g., woods v. grasslands, flat plains v. ridges, etc.). In both survey experiments, respondents were randomized into one of two branches. In Branch 1, respondents viewed only the process but they were not told the outcome of the policy siting decision. In Branch 2, respondents viewed both the process and the outcome. I also randomized the level of local control and public input. In Experiment 1, the “State board + limited input” condition presents a process where decisions are made by a state-appointed board of energy experts with limited, remote community input (“calling or writing an email

to your governor”). The other condition reflects the typical status quo where decisions over wind turbines are made by elected local legislators with high levels of face-to-face community input (“attending local meetings and voicing your concerns face-to-face with your elected legislators”).

In Experiment 2, I decouple this treatment into a level of government and level of public input treatments which vary independently of each other. For level of government, the conditions are:

• “your locally elected legislators”

• “a state-appointed board of energy experts”

For public input, the conditions are:

• “hold a series of local public meetings where residents like you can share your opinions face-to-face with the decision makers”

• “set up an email account where residents like you can send your thoughts, but there will be no public meetings about the proposal”

Finally, I randomized whether the proposed projected is approved or denied by its respective decision-making body. This randomized outcome was only visible for respondents in Branch 2, where the outcome is known. The survey flows for each experiment are diagrammed in Figures SI1 and SI3, as well as the stylized survey flow in Figure 1.

The primary outcomes of interest are the fairness of the wind turbine siting process and the legitimacy of the final decision. For fairness, the survey asks: “Regardless of project approval, do you believe this is a fair way to decide on where to permit new wind energy farms?” Respondents indicated the fairness on a 7-point scale from “very unfair” to “very fair.” I operationalize this outcome on a continuous scale from 0 to 1.

For legitimacy, I use an original four item scale of statement with agreement ranging on a 5-points scale from ”strongly agree” to ”strongly disagree.” I average the four items. The four items are internally consistent with a Cronbach’s alpha of .70. See SI Section 5 for validation of the legitimacy scale. The items are as follows, with the order randomized:

“Thinking about the process and [eventual / NA] decision, do you agree or disagree with each of the following statements?”

• “This decision ought to be accepted and considered the final word on the matter.”

• “The body that made this decision had the right to do so.”

• “There ought to be an effort to challenge the decision and get it changed.” (reversecoded)

• “Even if I personally disagree, I respect the outcome of this process.”

Experiment 1 uses a compound treatment of state-appointed board with limited public input in the form of written comments compared to locally elected officials with high public input in the form of face-to-face meetings. Respondents told that the decision would be made by a state-appointed board with limited public input were 10 percentage points (d = 0.37) less likely to perceive the decision-making process as fair. This effect is larger among those who tend to mostly agree with policies decisions made by their local government (SI Section 6). This 10 point effect is unchanged even when controlling for pretreatment support for the wind farm, concern for climate change, and the wind farm’s expected effects on local property values (Table S6).

Experiment 2 decouples Experiment 1’s compound treatment into separate treatments for the level of decision-making and public input. Having the decision made by a stateappointed board decreases fairness by 3 points (d = 0.11), compared to a decision by local elected officials. Holding no public meetings and only relying on written public comment decreases fairness by 9 points (d = 0.29), compared to holding in-person meetings. The difference between these effects is statistically significant.

Experiment 2 also extends the effect of process to outcome legitimacy. Having the decision made by a state-appointed board of experts decreases legitimacy by 2 points (d = 0.12) and holding no public meetings decreases legitimacy by 3 points (d = 0.14). Altogether, Experiment 2 shows that the negative effects on fairness from Experiment 1 are driven primarily by the low level of public input, though both process treatments have comparable effects on outcome legitimacy. That the effect of process on legitimacy is smaller compared to that of fairness is expected. As stated above, legitimacy is a combination of one’s baseline support for the outcome combined with their evaluation of procedural fairness (Tyler 1994).

### Resilience of Effects When Outcome Is Known

Having established that process affects fairness and legitimacy absent knowledge of the outcome, I test whether people are similarly responsive to process when the outcome is known,

reflecting sincere procedural values. Figure 3 shows the interaction between process and branch—whether the respondent knows the final decision. Across both experiments, knowing the outcome does not alter the effect of process on fairness and legitimacy. Whether or not the outcome is known, respondents still believe that the decision-making process is less fair and less legitimate when the decision is made by the state-appointed board or with limited public input. These null findings indicate process is having a sincere—not purely strategic—effect on respondents’ judgment prior to knowing outcomes.

One concern may be that the null results in Figure 3 stem from respondents not internalizing the outcome. To test this theory, I interact the process treatments with an indicator for whether the outcome matches the respondent’s pretreatment stated preference for the wind farm—either that it would be approved or denied. Figure 4 shows that receiving one’s preferred outcome dramatically increases a respondent’s perception of fairness and decision legitimacy by 11 to 16 percentage points, respectively. Respondents are internalizing the outcome and yet the outcome does not alter the independent effect of process. Nor does getting one’s preferred outcome alter the effect of process in a consistent manner (SI Section 7). However, Figure 4 also underscores the limited effects of process. These process effects are substantially smaller than the effect of getting one’s preferred policy.

## Discussion

Across two experiments, this study identifies a sincere effect of process on procedural fairness. Face-to-face public input and local control increase perceived fairness and legitimacy. These effects persist even after individuals learn whether they have received their preferred outcome. Procedural evaluations are therefore not merely strategic projections of anticipated outcomes, but rather they reflect stable attitudes toward how decisions ought to be made. One potential concern is that the null interactions in Figure 3 reflect insufficient statistical power rather than genuine resilience of process effects. Section J address this concern with a one-sided test against the full-strategic prediction: that the interaction coefficient equals or exceeds the main process effect in magnitude, indicating complete attenuation once the outcome is known. Three of five models reject this null at conventional levels, with a fourth model falling to reject at p = .053. The exceptions are both state board treatments, the smallest main effects of the study, stressing statistical power. Still, the consistent pattern of near zero interactions across all models is broadly consistent with sincere procedural values. Another concern is whether the effects of process vary based on whether the voter received their preferred outcome. Section G investigates this in an non-preregistered exploratory analysis. In four of five treatments, receiving one’s preferred outcome does not alter the negative process effects shown in Figure 2. The exception is the state board treatment on fairness in Experiment 2, where those who receive their preferred outcome are no longer sensitive to who made the decision. One interpretation is that policy winners are able to ignore more ambiguous aspects of procedural fairness such as who made the decision, whereas public input is seen as uniformly fair in an way that cannot be ignored. Given the risk of a false positive and the absence of this pattern in Experiment 1, I interpret this as suggestive rather than conclusive and an avenue for future studies.

Applied to the political process, the presence of sincere procedural values does not mean that all public testimony prior to learning the outcome is sincere. People may bring in all sorts of considerations and motivations that they couch in the language of procedural fairness, noise that this study cannot fully account for. Likewise, changes in process do affect people’s expectations of the likely policy outcome (see Section H). However, within these testimonies is a resilient effect of the process on fairness and legitimacy. In other words,

changes in perceived fairness based on the process are not purely strategic considerations driven by motivated reasoning.

As for governance, state and national governments across North American and Europe are increasingly preempting local control to facilitate the supply of new renewable energy. On one hand, these findings urge caution. This loss of local control and public input will make voters somewhat less accepting of the siting decisions. Over time, this depressed legitimacy risks degrading general trust in government and exacerbating right-wing populist backlash already associated with decarbonization (Bosetti et al. 2025).

On the other hand, ex post evaluations of fairness are mainly driven by whether voters received their preferred outcome, thus introducing bias in past efforts to study these risks. Furthermore, the loss of legitimacy from centralization is modest. Such reforms may not lose many would-be supporters due to procedural fairness. Instead, most project opponents would have opposed the siting outcome regardless of the process. In all, the political losses from centralization are likely less consequential than the ability of these reforms to expedite the deployment of renewable energy.

Still, this study measures average treatment effects within the entire sample. If these modest average effects are larger among a subset of highly engaged, well-resources opponents, then that faction may be influential enough to stymie the deployment of renewable energy or even reverse the centralizing reforms (see Norway above). Future research should assess whether these relatively modest losses of legitimacy translate into behavioral backlash and meaningful consequences for renewable energy deployment. This study provides a framework for such an evaluation, clarifying when process meaningfully sustains legitimacy compared to when it merely reflects underlying disagreements over outcomes.

## C Turbine Height Implications

Modern onshore wind turbines have an average hub height of 339 feet (Hartman 2024). Unfortunately, I interpreted that hub height as a tip of blade height in my visualization of the proposed turbines. How may this error have affected the experimental results? Taller turbines are likely to lead to more local opposition, but it is unclear if taller turbines would alter the legitimizing effect of the approval process. Instead, my expectation is that taller turbines would lead to an intercept shift downward—less support and less legitimacy overall—but the process conditions would have similar legitimizing treatment effects.

## D Descriptive Statistics

Tables D-1 and D-2 compare each experiment’s sample to its relevant population. Fielded on Cloud Research Connect, Experiment 1 sampled rural and suburban ZIP codes. To define the comparison group, I use the ZIP codes from which at least one respondent has entered Experiment 1’s sample. These ZIP code traits are weighted by their population to reflect the composition of residents themselves.

Experiment 1’s sample reflects the population’s composition of homeownership, gender, and age. Respondents in the sample have slightly lower incomes than the comparison population and are more likely to be white and college educated. The sample is 30% Republican or leans Republican. While partisanship data is not captured in the US Census, I believe that the ZIP population of these areas is slightly more Republican than the sample.

Experiment 2 samples exclusively rural ZIP codes and used demographic quotas based on the ZIP code data. While PureSpectrum has a deeper pool of rural respondents, these quotas were not strict. Still, the sample reflects the rural target population in terms of homeownership, college education, and income. The sample has an over-representation of women, white respondents, and older respondents. The sample is majority (54%) Republican/Republican leaners which is a reasonable target.

Although both samples exhibit modest deviations from their target populations on several demographic traits, these imbalances are unlikely to bias the estimated treatment effects. The treatments in both experiments are fully randomized at the individual level, and all observed demographic traits are controlled for in the analysis. As a result, differences between the sample and the target population may affect the levels of outcomes but not the internal validity of causal comparisons across treatment conditions (Coppock and McClellan 2019).

## F Alignment with Local Government

One explanation for the effect of level of decision making is that respondents may feel poorly represented by their state government. To test this mechanism, Experiment 1 asked: “How often do you agree with the decisions made by your local government?” I standardize this outcome as a two standard deviation effect to make it akin to a binary variable (Gelman 2008).

Of course, some respondents may feel poorly represented at the state and local level. Experiment 2 instead asked respondents to evaluate their agreement with decisions by their local government and state government. I subtract agreement with state government with agreement with local government to generate a cleaner measure of preference for local government control.

Figure F-8 shows the interactive effect of process with a preference for local government. In Experiment 1, for those who prefer local government, the compound treatment of state board with limited input suggests an even greater decrease in perceived fairness (p = .07), though this measurement of local government preference has flaws. In Experiment 2, for those who prefer local government, a decision made by state board of experts has a similar negative effect of fairness and legitimacy (p < .05). Of course, one’s preference for local government is not randomly assigned, so these respondents may differ in other ways I cannot account for.

## G Outcome Matches and Perceptions

Figure G-9 shows the interaction between match and the process. In 4 out of 5 treatments, getting one’s preferred outcome does not alter the negative effects of process found in Figure

### 1. However, in Experiment 2, the interaction between outcome match and state board is

large enough to negate the negative effect of state board. This suggests that people who receive their preferred outcome are no longer sensitive to who made the decision when judging the fairness of the process. However, those who did not receive their preferred outcome still perceive the state board as less fair than the local elected officials.

It is unclear why this may be the case and I hesitate to overanalyze one interaction from one of the treatments due to the risk of a false positive. Though, for theory generation, perhaps the level of decision maker is fungible in a way that the amount of public input is not. It may be easier to discount a state board as either corrupt or unimportant depending on the outcome compared to discounting public input. Public input may be seen as uniformly fair, regardless of the outcome. That this interaction exists in Experiment 2 but there is no evidence of it in Experiment 1 suggests that more data is needed to understand the stability and meaning of the relationship.

## H Expected Outcomes

In Experiment 2, respondents in both Branches 1 and 2 stated their expectations of whether the proposed wind far would be approved or denied following the description of the approval process. Thus, I use both branches in evaluating the effect of process on expectations of outcome. There is little evidence that the process systematically affects expectations of approval (Figure H-10).

However, Figure H-11 displays these effects based on respondents’ pretreatment support for the wind proposal. Surprisingly, respondents differ in their perceptions of how public input will affect the outcome. Pro-wind respondents believe that less public input will decrease the likelihood of approval, whereas anti-wind respondents believe the opposite. Perhaps both groups believe their neighbors share their attitudes, meaning more public input will secure their preferred outcome.

To be clear, that process affects perceptions of the outcome does not negate the independent effect of process on fairness and legitimacy. Respondents may expect different outcomes based on the process, but these outcomes still do not alter the legitimizing effects of procedural fairness.

## J One-Sided Test

Table J-13 reports one-sided tests of the full “strategic considerations” null—that the interaction coefficient equals or exceeds the main effect in magnitude, indicating complete attenuation of the process effect once the outcome is known. Three of five models reject this null at conventional levels. For the two largest effects—the compound treatment on fairness in Experiment 1 (β = 0.020, p < .001) and limited public input on fairness in Experiment 2 (β = -0.006, p < .001)—the interactions cluster near zero and are statistically distinguishable from full strategic attenuation despite the relatively modest main effects. Limited public input on legitimacy similarly rejects the strategic null (β = −0.002, p = .018).

The two exceptions are both state board treatments. State board on fairness marginally fails to reject (p = .053), and state board on legitimacy fails more clearly (p = .235). However, both have the smallest main effects in the study (-3.3 and -2.4 percentage points, respectively). Even complete strategic contamination of effects this small would be substantively inconsequential for the paper’s conclusions. The failure to reject the strategic null for these models therefore largely reflects the inherent difficulty of distinguishing near zero effects from other near zero effects, not affirmative evidence for strategic motivation.

Taken together, the pattern is consistent with sincere procedural values: interactions are near zero across all five models, the three largest effects clearly reject full strategic attenuation, and the two exceptions are driven by main effects too small to draw strong inferences in either direction.
